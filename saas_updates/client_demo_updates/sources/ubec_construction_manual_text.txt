PREFACE

The Universal Basic Education Commission (UBEC) is proud to present the Construction Manual
for Basic Education Infrastructure. This document represents a fundamental cornerstone of our
mission to provide quality, accessible, and conducive learning environments for every Nigerian
child. The availability of safe, functional, and well-equipped school infrastructure is not merely
a matter of convenience; it is a prerequisite for achieving equitable and inclusive education for
all.

The Compulsory, Free Universal Basic Education Act of 2004 established the framework for a
nationwide revitalization of basic education. A critical component of this revitalization is the
provision and maintenance of high-quality school infrastructure, which directly impacts student
attendance, teacher retention, and overall learning outcomes. This manual has been
meticulously developed to serve as the definitive guide for all stakeholders involved in the
design, construction, and rehabilitation of basic education facilities.

This manual is designed for a diverse audience, including architects, engineers, contractors,
state education boards, local government authorities, and community leaders. It outlines the
minimum standards, technical specifications, and best practices for constructing new
classrooms, and providing essential amenities such as furniture, libraries, and laboratories. By
adhering to these guidelines, we can ensure consistency in quality, optimize the use of public
funds, and create a sustainable infrastructure that will serve generations to come.

We are deeply grateful for the technical expertise and collaborative spirit of all our partners,
including the State Universal Basic Education Boards (SUBEBs), professional bodies, and
development partners, whose invaluable contributions have made this comprehensive guide a
reality. This manual is a living document that will be updated periodically to reflect the latest
advancements in construction technology and sustainability.

It is our firm belief that investing in school infrastructure is one of the most impactful ways to
secure a brighter future for our children. We urge all stakeholders to embrace these standards
and work collectively to build a resilient and robust basic education system for Nigeria.

Thank you.

1.0 INTRODUCTION

The construction manual contains the technical specifications for the work to be done at di erent
stages of the project. It is a supporting document that the SBMC and Contractor shall rely on to
carry out the construction activities under the project. The construction manual is to be read
and used in conjunction with the approved working drawings.

The designs have been made for classroom blocks, multipurpose hall, Admin building,
Laboratory/ICT Room, WASH facilities and perimeter fence etc. They are to be built with sand
Crete blocks, Compressed Stabilized Earth Bricks, Burnt Clay Bricks etc.

The Construction manual and the technical specifications contained in it have been made in
such a way that it can be understood by both technical and non-technical people engaged in the
construction activities. The objective of the Construction Manual is to ensure that the buildings
are constructed as specified in the drawings and to the quality required.

The construction manual has been laid out to follow the construction process from site
investigation and takeover; use of Materials tools, ironmongery, masonry and concrete works,
carpentry, roofing to finishing works.

1.1 RESPONSIBILITIES

The Department of Physical Planning of the participating SUBEBs and LGEAs shall be largely
responsible for the supervision of the projects. The staff involved shall have the technical
knowledge to guide the contractors towards realizing the objectives of the projects.

The National UBEC Physical Planning Department shall be responsible for the Approval of all the
SIPs, Drawings, Specifications and BOQs

Due to the close relationship between design and construction, the construction of the schools
should rely heavily on the working drawings and documents. These include detailed architecture,
structural, M&E drawings and bill of quantities. The drawings must be followed strictly. However,
where any deviation arises, approval must be obtained from UBEC Physical Planning Unit.

1.2 What is SBMC-SIP?

The School-Based Management Committee (SBMC) is a community­school governance structure
established to strengthen partnership, accountability, and local ownership of school improvement.
As explained in the SBMC­SIP Implementation Manual, global experience shows that when school
communities are actively involved in planning and managing physical improvements, schools
achieve better outcomes. The SBMC serves as a bridge between the school and the community,
ensuring that construction and renovation projects are not seen as external impositions, but as
community-owned assets.

In the context of this manual covering construction, renovation, and rehabilitation, the SBMC is vital
because physical interventions require:

     Agreed Priorities: Deciding which classrooms or blocks require the most urgent structural
         attention.

     Oversight and Quality Assurance: Providing local monitoring to ensure that contractors or
         voluntary labor meet the technical specifications outlined in this manual.

     Protection of Assets: Mobilizing the community to prevent vandalism and theft of
         construction materials and finished buildings.

     Maintenance Planning: Establishing a routine schedule to ensure that new roofs, floors, and
         walls do not fall back into disrepair.

1.3 School Improvement Programme (SIP)

The School Improvement Programme (SIP) is a key pillar for improving education quality by identifying
a school's academic and priority goals. Within this framework, the SBMC­SIP approach supports the
execution of community-initiated priority projects. This manual provides the technical roadmap for
the infrastructure component of the SIP, specifically:

     Construction Works: Building additional classrooms, libraries, and perimeter fencing to
         expand access.

     Renovation/Rehabilitation: Re-roofing, flooring, and finishing dilapidated or abandoned
         structures to restore a conducive learning environment.

By situating these physical works within the SIP process, this manual ensures that every brick laid
and every roof repaired contributes to a long-term, community-led vision for school development.

2.0 PRE-CONSTRUCTION ACTIVITIES

2.1 SITE INVESTIGATION AND SURVEY
The identified sites for the schools shall have a Condition Survey done for Topographic(to
determine the accurate natural and man-made features, elevations, and contours of the site)
and Geotechnical (to determine the physical and mechanical properties of the soil and rock
beneath the surface) Investigations to confirm or identify stability and potential problems. The
results from these shall be used by the physical planning departments of the participatory states
to adapt the prototype designs to the specific site conditions of the project sites with reference
to the foundation designs. The results shall also help to indicate the location of the proposed
buildings on the site.

2.2 SITE PREPARATION
Prior to construction, the site shall be prepared to include the following:
Temporary Fencing or Barriers to be erected around the building site from the existing school to
enable them to function without hindrance, for safety concerns.
Identifying areas to be used for temporary accommodation like the site o ice, storage shed in
addition to areas for waste storage and disposal.
Identifying and preparing areas for the storage of materials like cement, aggregates and other
construction materials.

2.3 STORAGE OF MATERIALS
Materials for the Construction shall be stored in Safe Place

2.4 SITE SECURITY
The primary objectives of site security shall be:
Security against theft.
Security from opportunistic vandals.
Each site has its own unique requirement for security. Perimeter fencing, internal site
protection, day and night security may all be necessary.

3.0 CONSTRUCTION MATERIALS
The major materials to be used include Water, Cement, Soft and Hard aggregates, Sand Crete
blocks, Compressed Stabilized Earth Bricks, Burnt Clay Bricks, Reinforcement Bars and Wire
mesh.
Others include Timber which shall be used for Formwork and Roofing, Aluminum Roofing Sheets
and Steel for Windows and Doors.
The main material for the finishes shall be Gloss and Emulsion Paint on plastered walls for Sand
Crete blocks; Lacquer for burnt clay bricks and Stabilized Earth Blocks. Tyrolean and Textured
Paints should also be used.

3.1 WATER
The quality of water used in construction has a direct impact on the strength of important
elements in the works.
Water used in construction should have the following properties:
It should be potable [fit for human consumption]
It should be clean and free from impurities.
It should be transparent, colorless and odorless.
There should be no vegetable growth or organic materials.
It should not appear oily nor contain salt.
It should not smell bad nor have a bitter taste.

3.2 CEMENT
Cement shall be Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) or Pozzolana Cement.
Cement shall be delivered to site in sound and properly sealed bags of 50kg weight; legibly
marked with the manufacturer's name and registered trademark. They shall be well protected
during transit and in storage. The cement bags shall be stored in dry, leak and moisture proof
storage areas. Cement in damaged or broken bags shall not be used in the construction works.

3.3 BLOCKS

3.3.1 SANDCRETE BLOCKS
Hollow sand Crete blocks are precast masonry units made from a mixture of cement, sand,
and water, molded with hollow cores to reduce weight and improve insulation. They must meet
dimensional, strength, and durability standards set by building codes such as BS 6073 (1981)
and Nigerian Industrial Standards (NIS 87:2000)
Standard sizes (NIS 87:2000):

      450 mm × 225 mm × 225 mm (9-inch block)
      450 mm × 225 mm × 150 mm (6-inch block)
      450 mm × 225 mm × 100 mm (4-inch block)

Blocks shall be laid in stretcher bond with mortar joints binding them together

      Figure 3: Sample of Vibrated Hollow Sand Crete Block 450mm × 225mm x 225mm (9-inch block)

3.3.2 COMPRESSED STABILIZED EARTH BLOCKS (CSEB)
Compressed Stabilized Earth Blocks (CSEBs) under specification 3.3.2 are soil-based masonry
units stabilized with cement or lime, mechanically compressed, and cured to achieve strength
and durability. Shall be Solid Rectangular Blocks. Blocks come in 290 mm × 140 mm × 90 mm (varies
by press type); blocks shall be laid stretcher bond with mortar joints binding them together.

3.3.3 INTERLOCKING STABILIZED SOIL BLOCKS (ISSB)
Interlocking Stabilized Soil Blocks (ISSBs) are defined as compressed soil blocks stabilized with
cement or lime, designed with grooves and projections that allow them to interlock without thick
mortar joints. Blocks come in 140mm x 290mm x 95mm, as Solid Rectangular Blocks with
interlocking features; blocks shall be laid by dry stacking method.

Clay bricks are made from Natural clay or shale, sometimes with additives like sand or fly ash,
Molded or extruded, dried, and fired in kilns at 900­1,100°C. Clay bricks can be solid or hollow core.
Hollow core clay bricks of 310mm x 175mm x 145mm shall be used in the Construction and laid in
stretcher bond with mortar joints binding them together.

4.0 CONSTRUCTION ACTIVITIES
The Construction Activities will focus on the Setting Out, Substructure, Super Structure and
Finishing Works

SETTING OUT
The setting out shall be done using the building plans and necessary material like pegs, nails, line,
tape etc. The first task to set is to establish a baseline which serves as a reference from which all
the corners of the building are located on the ground. The position of the baseline should be
clearly marked out on-site so that it can be reestablished when required. A long, sturdy tape
should be used for on-site measurements [30m would be suitable].
After the baseline has been set, marked and checked, the main lines of the building can be set
out. Each corner of the building shall be marked with a nail on the horizontal timber The setting out
shall be carried out by a Site Engineer.

Figure 11: Sample of Building Setting Out

All Centre lines and wall lines shall be marked with nails and clearly labelled. The setting out
lines shall be checked for right angles and correct lengths to avoid errors. This can be done by
using 3-4-5 method which is an application of the Pythagoras theorem.

After the setting out of the building lines has been completed and checked, the contractor shall
ensure that the positions are transferred to the ground; using a spirit level to ensure that it is
plumb. This layout of trenches establishes the excavation size, shape, direction as well as the
width and position of walls. This should be double checked for accuracy before excavation for
foundation trenches and column bases can begin. For excavation, the sides of the trenches

6.0 FINISHES

6.1 PLASTERING

The plaster surface shall be relatively smooth and finished true and plumb to the degree of
smoothness specified. The sand for the plaster shall be tested for impurities, silt content and
particle size. Only a small proportion of clay can be tolerated in sand used in plaster. The soil
content shall be less than 8% and an accurate mix ratio must be used. A suitable measuring
container for the plaster ingredients should be used.

To ensure the quality of the plaster mix, using a mixer machine is preferable if not essential. However,
where this is not available, hand mixing can be done on a clean, non-absorbent area; that is not
directly on the ground nor contaminated with soil. Care must be taken to mix the dry cement and
sand not less than three times to get all the materials finely blended to a uniform color.

Water shall be added slowly until the mix reaches a consistency suitable for plaster. Note that as
little water as possible should be used to bring the plaster to a consistency that makes it possible to
be applied easily. Excess water may cause trouble due to segregation, di iculty to apply, loss of
plaster droppings and excessive shrinkage. The water used for plaster should be potable [fit for
drinking].

The plaster is to be used shortly after mixing; within 30 - 40 minutes of adding water. Before any
plastering starts, all surfaces shall be prepared to assure uniform thickness of plaster. Dust, loose
materials and mortar that can interfere with bonding should be removed. The surface to be plastered
shall also be cleaned and kept wet before plastering commences. Plaster thickness should be
between 12mm and 15mm and as uniform as possible. Plasters should be cured for several days;
two to three times daily until the plaster is hardened.

6.2 JOINING BRICKWORK.

In exposed masonry, joints provide spots and areas from where rainwater and dampness can get into
the building. Jointing is the finishing of mortar joints in brick masonry construction. It is a process of
removing semi set mortar to a depth of 10mm to 20mm and filing it with fresh mortar in desired shape.
If needed, the fresh mortar could contain a colouring pigment to enhance the brickwork.

In this project, the brickwork shall be finished with recessed jointing. This shall be done at the same
time as the brickwork. The joints are filled with mortar and then pushed back to 5mm or more using
the appropriate tools. The excess mortar is removed and the surface is finished and cured for about
ten days

7.0 QUALITY ASSURANCES

7.1 CEMENT
Cement is a key ingredient in making concrete. It is often necessary to perform cement quality tests
on-site to ensure that the quality of cement delivered to the site is adequate.These tests should
include the following:

Date of Manufacturing - The strength of cement reduces with age, therefore, the date of
manufacturing of cement on the bags should be checked.

Cement Color - The color of cement should be uniform. It should be typical cement
colour which is grey colour with a light greenish shade.

Hard Lumps formed - Cement should be free from hard lumps which are usually formed
by the absorption of moisture from the atmosphere. Where lumps are found and they do
not become powdery when rubbed between the fingers, the cement is already set and
should be discarded.

Temperature inside cement Bag - If the hand is plunged into a bag of cement, it should
be cool inside the cement bag.

Smoothness Test- Touching and rubbing cement between the fingers should give a smooth
feeling. Roughness indicates adulteration with sand.

Water Sinking Test- If a small quantity of cement is thrown into the water, it should float
some time before finally sinking.

7.2 TESTING COARSE AGGREGATE
Aggregates should be tested to ascertain their quality and suitability for particular types of
concrete work. Before conducting any test on aggregates, the first thing to do is sampling.
Sampling can also be used to determine the quality of aggregates to be used. It is the most
important step in ensuring quality. A sample should truly represent the total material being
checked. There are several methods of sampling, and they include:

7.2.1 STOCKPILES
Obtaining unbiased samples from stockpiles is often di icult due to segregation of materials
when they have been stockpiled. Suitable power equipment like a bucket loader will make the
process easier and give a better representative sample as the loader can get samples from
di erent layers and angles of the stockpile. These can be combined and mixed in a smaller pile to
form the sample.

UBEC ­ Construction, Renovation & Wash Manual  Page 36 of 114
Part I. Construction Manual

However, if the sampling has to be done manually, materials should be taken at least in three
increments: from the top, middle and bottom of the pile. A board should be inserted vertically
into the pile just above the sampling point to help in preventing more material from falling into
the sampling area. The outer layer should be removed and the increments from the material left
collected with a shovel. Before testing, the field sample shall be reduced to appropriate testing
sizes either by splitting or quartering.

7.2.2 SAMPLING DIRECTLY FROM A TRUCK
Sampling from a truck is not recommended because factors like segregation or particle
breakdown during transportation, loading and unloading may influence the gradation of the
material. Therefore, such sampling should be done at the point of delivery.
To do this, three portions of material should be taken from locations across the truck dump: one
portion from the front corner, one portion from the opposite corner and another portion from
the top of the dumped load. Take additional material from the Centre of the dumped load if
needed to meet the minimum sample size. The portions should be mixed to form a
representative sample from the truck.
For fine aggregates, a shovel or sampling tube should be used to obtain approximately equal
portions from six or more locations. The portions should be combined to form a composite
sample.

7.2.4 DETERMINATION OF FLAKINESS INDEX

Flakiness and the shape of coarse aggregate have an appreciable e ect on the work ability of
concrete.

Flakiness is considered a source of weakness. Flaky aggregate is defined as an aggregate
particle with a least thickness less than 0.6 of the mean of the smallest sieve size through which
the particle passes and the largest sieve size on which the particle is retained. The flaky index of
a material is the percentage by weight o laky particles ina sample.

The purpose of the test for flakiness is to determine the flakiness index of a sample of coarse
aggregate.

The test is not applicable to sizes smaller than 6.3mm. The test is carried out using a specified
metal thickness gauge. A given sample of aggregates; each fraction at least a minimum of 200
pieces can be used. The pieces of aggregate are made to pass through the gauge and weighed.
The total amount should be weighed to an accuracy of 0.1% of the weight of the samples taken.
The flakiness index is calculated by expressing the weight of flaky particles as a percentage of
the total weight of the sample. The maximum allowable limit of the flaky particles in the mix is 30%.
If it is more than this, then the mix is not suitable for construction purposes.

7.3 TESTING FINE AGGREGATE

Fine aggregates are principally sand. They are small size filler materials in construction. They
are particles that pass through 4.75mm sieve and retain on 0.075mm sieve. They fill the voids
between the coarse aggregate in concrete and bulk the concrete, helping to increase the
workability of concrete.

Fine aggregate to be used in concrete and masonry works should be well graded, clean - free
from lumps, organic matter etc. It should be free from clay, mica and soft flaky particles. It
should also be strong, angular and durable, not react with cement after mixing and should not
absorb more than 5% of water. For any concrete mix, the specific gravity a ine aggregate should
be about 2.7.

Sand can come as fine sand or coarse sand. Fine sand has 90% of its particles size between
0.25mm and 0.06mm while coarse sand has 90% of its particles size between 2.0mm and
0.5mm.

There are simple tests that can be carried out to determine the quality of sand on site. For example,
to test the presence of clay in the sand, the following procedure should be followed:

A glass should be¾ filled with water and sand added to it.

It should be vigorously shaken and the sand allowed to settle.

The glass should be checked if a layer has been formed on the sand.

The thickness of the layer should indicate the percentage of clay present in the sand. Good
quality sand should have less than 8% of clay in it. A quick way to test clay is to hold some dry
sand and drop it. If the sand sticks to your palm, then it contains clay.

4.3. TIMBER TRUSSES
  Rehabilitating timber trusses is a delicate but essential process to restore structural
  integrity, especially in aging buildings or those exposed to moisture and termite damage.

  It is a multi-step process that requires thorough assessment, proper repair techniques,
  and preventative measures. It is often a task for a professional structural engineer or
  contractor, especially for significant damage.

  Here is a general breakdown of the process:

4.3.1. INITIAL ASSESSMENT AND DIAGNOSIS
  The trusses shall be adequately braced to stabilize the building and to ensure a
  satisfactory roof line for completion; the supporting structure must be plumbed to
  subsequently achieve a level ceiling line. Care must be taken to ensure that the
  supporting structures, like the walls and beams, are adequately designed and stable.

  The first and most crucial step is to identify the root cause and extent of the damage.
  This involves a visual inspection and, for more complex cases, non-destructive testing
  (NDT) to evaluate the timber's integrity. Common issues to look for include:

   Moisture Damage: This is the most common cause of failure, leading to rot and decay.
       Signs include discoloration, a musty odor, or soft, spongy wood.

   Insect Infestation: Termites, carpenter ants, and other wood-boring insects can
       compromise the structural integrity of the wood. Look for sawdust, hollow-sounding
       areas, or visible galleries.

   Structural Failures: This can be caused by improper installation, overloading, or natural
       aging. Signs include sagging, cracking, splitting, or joint failure.

Figure 18: Sample Image of Initial Assessment of Timber Roof Truss

UBEC ­ Construction, Renovation & Wash Manual  Page 64 of 114
   Part II. Renovation Manual

4.3.2. REPAIR & REINFORCEMENT TECHNIQUE
  Based on the assessment, a specific repair method is chosen. The goal is to restore the
  truss to its original strength and capacity.
   Replacing Damaged Sections: For localized damage such as rot or decay, the a ected
       portion of the timber is cut out and replaced with a new piece of timber. The new piece
       is then joined to the existing truss using Scarfe joints, which are secured with bolts,
       screws, or high-tensile steel bars.

                           Figure 19: Sample Image of Repair of Damaged Section of Timber Roof Truss

 Reinforcement with Steel Plates: For more severe damage or to strengthen a
     weakened area, steel plates can be used. These plates are bolted or welded onto the
     timber members, providing additional support and stability. This is an e ective and cost-
     e icient method for reinforcing key joints.

Figure 20: Sample Image of Reinforcement with Steel Plates.

UBEC ­ Construction, Renovation & Wash Manual                Page 65 of 114
   Part II. Renovation Manual

   Epoxy Resin Injection: For cracks or splits, a special epoxy resin can be injected into
       the fissures. The resin fills the crack and hardens, creating a strong bond that restores
       the timber's strength without the need for major replacement.

   Adding Bracing: Additional diagonal or horizontal braces can be installed to provide
       extra support and distribute the load more evenly across the truss members, especially
       in cases of sagging.

4.3.3. PROTECTIVE TREATMENT & PREVENTION
  Once the repairs are complete, it is essential to protect the truss from future damage.

   Moisture Control: This is critical. Ensure the roof has proper ventilation and that there
       are no leaks. A moisture barrier or a water-resistant coating can be applied to the timber.

   Pest Control: Applying insect-resistant treatments to the wooden members can prevent
       future infestations.

   Regular Inspections: Routine inspections (at least twice a year) are key to catching
       small issues before they become major problems.

4.3.4. MATERIALS & TOOLS FOR REPAIRS
   New Timber: For replacing decayed, rotted, or insect-damaged sections, It's crucial to
       use timber of the same species and moisture content as the original to prevent future
       warping and ensure a strong bond.

   Steel Plates and Fasteners: Metal plates (gussets) are used to reinforce weakened
       joints or to splice new timber sections to old ones. They are typically secured with bolts,
       screws, or nails.

                                Figure 21: Sample image reinforcement with steel plates.

 High-Quality Adhesives and Resins: Structural adhesives, such as epoxy resin, are
     injected into cracks and fissures to bond the timber back together. This is a non-invasive
     way to restore strength to split or cracked members.

Part II. Renovation Manual

                                           Figure 22: Sample Image of High-Quality Adhesives

 Steel Rods: For more significant structural reinforcement, steel rods can be used,
     particularly in combination with epoxy resin injection to strengthen the timber and
     provide additional load-bearing capacity.

                                           Figure 23: Sample Image of Steel Rods

 Wood Preservatives: These are essential for preventing rot, decay, and insect

10.5. BOREHOLE REHABILITATION
  Rehabilitation of borehole infrastructure in Nigerian schools is a critical and specialized
  undertaking. It involves a systematic approach to diagnose the problem, implement a
  solution, and ensure the long-term sustainability of the water source. This process is
  essential to move from the common cycle of borehole failure to a reliable, safe, and clean
  water supply for students and sta .

  Here is a detailed breakdown of the process:

10.5.1. INITIAL ASSESSMENT & DIAGNOSIS
  This is the most crucial step, as it determines the feasibility and scope of the rehabilitation.
  It requires a professional to identify the root cause of the failure.

   Consultation: Engage with school management, teachers, and students to understand
       the history of the borehole. Questions to ask include: When was it last functional? What
       were the first signs of failure (e.g., reduced yield, muddy water, pump noise)?

   Physical Inspection: A visual inspection of the above-ground components is
       performed. This includes checking the wellhead, hand pump (if applicable), power
       source (solar panels, generator), and any visible pipes or fittings for damage.

   Downhole Investigation: For a comprehensive diagnosis, a professional (e.g., a
       hydrogeologist or borehole expert) will use specialized tools. This may include:

               o Submersible Camera: A camera is lowered into the borehole to visually
                    inspect the casing, screens, and the bottom of the well for signs of damage,
                    silt, or blockages.

               o Pumping Test: This involves measuring the water level and flow rate under
                    specific conditions to determine the aquifer's yield and the borehole's
                    performance. A decline in performance is a key indicator of a problem.

               o Water Quality Test: A water sample is taken and tested for chemical,
                    physical, and biological parameters. Common issues include high iron
                    content, turbidity, and bacterial contamination.

   Part II. Renovation Manual

10.5.2. IDENTIFYING THE CAUSES OF FAILURE
  Based on the diagnosis, the cause of the borehole failure can be determined. Common
  causes in Nigeria include:

   Mechanical Failure: This is often related to the pump or pipework. It could be due to a
       worn-out pump, a broken pipe, or a damaged riser.

   Physical Clogging: Sediment, sand, or silt from the aquifer can enter the borehole,
       filling the well and blocking the screen. This is a very common problem, especially in
       older or poorly constructed boreholes.

   Chemical and Biological Issues:

               o Incrustation: The buildup of mineral deposits like iron or calcium carbonate
                    on the well screens and casing can reduce water flow.

               o Biofouling: The growth of bacteria and other microorganisms can create a
                    slimy film that clogs the well screen.

   Hydrological Failure: This is when the aquifer itself is no longer producing enough
       water. This could be due to over-pumping, seasonal changes, or a poorly sited borehole
       from the start.

10.5.3. DEVELOPING & EXECUTING THE REHABILITATION PLAN
  The rehabilitation plan is customized based on the identified cause of failure. The goal is to
  restore the borehole's capacity to produce water.

       Mechanical Cleaning (Jetting and Brushing): For physical clogging, high-pressure
           jetting with water or compressed air is used to blast sediment and debris from the
           borehole. A specialized brush may also be used to scrub the casing and screen.

       Chemical Treatment: If incrustation or biofouling is the problem, a chemical
           solution (often a mild acid or chlorine solution) is injected into the borehole to
           dissolve the mineral deposits or kill the bacteria. This requires precise calculation
           and handling by professionals.

       Pump and Pipe Repair/Replacement: The hand pump, submersible pump, and/or
           any damaged pipes are repaired or replaced.

       New Well Screen Installation: If the original well screen is severely damaged or
           corroded, a new, smaller-diameter screen can be installed inside the existing casing
           to act as a new water intake. This process is known as relining.

UBEC ­ Construction, Renovation & Wash Manual  Page 88 of 114
   Part II. Renovation Manual

       Re-development: After cleaning, the borehole is "developed" by pumping it at a high
           rate to remove any remaining debris and to stimulate the flow of water from the
           aquifer.

10.5.4. POST-REHABILITATION AND SUSTAINABILITY
  Rehabilitation is not a one-time fix. For long-term sustainability, a robust management and
  maintenance plan is essential.

       Disinfection: After all repairs are complete, the borehole must be disinfected using
           a chlorine solution to kill any bacteria introduced during the rehabilitation process.

       Final Water Quality Testing: A final water sample is taken to confirm that the water
           is safe for drinking.

       Formation of a School WASH Committee: The school should form a committee of
           teachers, students, and community members to oversee the borehole.

       Maintenance Fund: A system for collecting and managing a maintenance fund is
           critical. This could be a small contribution from the school or community to cover the
           costs of future repairs and spare parts.

       Training and Capacity Building: The WASH committee members are trained in
           routine maintenance, such as reporting problems early, basic repairs, and proper
           hygiene practices to prevent contamination.

       Regular Monitoring: Establish a schedule for periodic monitoring of the borehole's
           performance and water quality to catch issues before they escalate.

16.0 CONCLUSION

This manual is set out as a practical guide for carrying out construction works,
renovation/rehabilitation works, and WASH (Water, Sanitation and Hygiene) interventions
under the project. It is designed to support the School-Based Management Committee (SBMC),
the Project Implementation Sub-Committee (PISC), and designated supervisors in executing
all on-site activities correctly, efficiently, and to the highest standards of quality, safety, and
functionality.

It is important that this manual is not used in isolation, but as a supporting document throughout
planning, implementation, supervision, and completion of works. Accordingly, it should be read
and applied in conjunction with all relevant project documents, including:

     Approved working drawings and technical specifications, to ensure structural integrity,
         correct detailing, and compliance with required standards;

     SBMC­SIP implementation guidelines and the School Development Plan (SDP), to
         promote transparency, community ownership, accountability, and sustainability of project
         outcomes; and

     any other approved project instructions and records necessary for proper understanding,
         coordination, and implementation of activities on site.

By applying the guidance in this manual alongside the above documents, stakeholders will ensure
that every project deliverable--whether a newly constructed facility, a successfully
rehabilitated structure, or a functional water and sanitation service--contributes
meaningfully to a safe, healthy, inclusive, and conducive learning environment. Proper adherence
to these procedures will also strengthen long-term maintenance culture and help the community
protect and sustain the infrastructure and WASH facilities for lasting benefit.
